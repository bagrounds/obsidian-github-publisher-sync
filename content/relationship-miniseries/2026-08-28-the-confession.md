## The Confession  
  
The Thai restaurant was a study in sensory overload: the frantic clatter of chopsticks against ceramic, the thick, cloying scent of basil and chili, and the relentless, upbeat pop music thrumming through the walls. Ethan was already at their usual corner table, a bottle of Riesling open, his grin bright enough to catch the overhead lights.   
  
He looked like he belonged there. He looked like he belonged everywhere.  
  
Maya slid into the chair opposite him, her purse heavy on her shoulder, as if it contained the physical weight of her day. Ethan poured a glass of wine and slid it toward her, his movements fluid and practiced.   
  
Rough day, huh? he said, his tone expertly calibrated to soothe. I saw the Henderson email come through the server. Look, if you need a vent, I’m all ears. But seriously, it’s just one account. You’re the best designer they’ve got. They’re lucky to have you.  
  
He took a long sip of his wine, his eyes dancing toward the television mounted above the bar.   
  
Maya stared at the liquid in her glass. The wine was pale, translucent. She thought about the Henderson account, about the empty, hollow feeling in her chest when David had asked her who she was designing for. She realized that for three years, she had been designing for a version of herself that didn't exist—a person who was never tired, never confused, and never afraid.   
  
Ethan, she said. Her voice was quiet, barely audible over the music. I don't want to talk about the account.   
  
Ethan blinked, his smile wavering for a fraction of a second before he recovered. Okay. Sure. What’s up? Everything okay with the apartment?   
  
She gripped the stem of her glass until her knuckles turned white. She felt the urge to pivot, to make a joke about the leaky faucet or the neighbor’s loud dog, to return to the safe, shallow waters of their usual domestic script. But the crack in her chest was widening. The silence she had brought home from the office wasn't just an absence of noise; it was an invitation.   
  
I’m not the queen of pivots, she said.   
  
Ethan laughed, a short, reflexive sound. Babe, don't say that. You totally are. You handle everything.   
  
I don't handle anything, she interrupted, the words tumbling out, raw and jagged. I’m drowning, Ethan. I’m not smart, and I’m not organized, and I’m terrified that if I stop moving for even a second, you’re going to see that I’m just a fraud.   
  
She stopped, her breath hitching. The room seemed to tilt. She waited for him to dismiss it, to tell her she was being dramatic, to reach across the table and offer a quick, superficial touch.   
  
But Ethan had stopped looking at the television. He had stopped drinking his wine. He was leaning forward, his face stripped of the reflexive, polished mask he wore for the world. For the first time, he wasn't looking *past* her. He was looking *at* her.   
  
The silence between them wasn't empty. It was heavy, pulsing with the sudden, terrifying reality of the truth she had finally dared to speak. Maya felt a sharp, stinging heat behind her eyes. She had spent their entire marriage building a fortress to protect him from her failures, only to realize that the fortress was the very thing that had kept him from actually knowing her.   
  
She looked at her hands, trembling on the table. She had confessed the secret. The shame was there, visceral and hot, but beneath it, there was a strange, disorienting sense of relief. She had exposed the wound. Now, she would see if he would reach out to help her stitch it, or if he would simply turn away from the blood.   
  
Ethan didn't speak. He just kept looking at her, his expression unreadable, his armor, for the first time in their lives, left somewhere else entirely.
