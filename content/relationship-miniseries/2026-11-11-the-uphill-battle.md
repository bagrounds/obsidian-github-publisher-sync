# The Uphill Battle  
  
The cursor blinked on the screen—a rhythmic, mocking pulse. Clara stared at the site plan for the municipal library project, but the lines blurred into a frantic tangle of geometry. Her deadline was forty-eight hours away, and the structural integrity report was still a mess of unverified calculations.  
  
She shifted in her chair, the leather creaking in the otherwise tomb-like silence of the home office. Her shoulders felt as though they were supporting the entire weight of the ceiling. Every few seconds, her gaze flicked to the clock. Each minute that ticked away felt like a physical subtraction from her own capacity to function.  
  
She was cold, though the thermostat was set to seventy. She stood up, paced to the window, and looked out at the street. A neighbor was walking a dog. Two people were laughing as they ducked into a cafe across the way. They looked light, unencumbered, moving through the world with a buoyancy she hadn't felt in weeks.   
  
Clara leaned her forehead against the cool glass. She felt solitary in the most dangerous sense of the word—exposed, unanchored, and singularly responsible for a disaster that hadn't happened yet. Her brain was running at a fever pitch, scanning the environment for threats: *The budget gap. The zoning delay. The inspector’s skepticism.* She was performing the work of three people, her mind attempting to simulate every potential failure point simultaneously.  
  
She returned to the desk and tried to type, but her fingers felt clumsy, thick. The load was no longer just professional; it was physiological. A dull ache had settled behind her eyes, the familiar sensation of a nervous system redlining.   
  
If I can just get the load-bearing calculations finished, she told herself, the voice in her head sounding thin and brittle. But the numbers wouldn't align. She felt a phantom pressure on her chest, a weight that wasn't there, yet felt heavy enough to crush the breath out of her.  
  
She looked at her phone. No messages. The silence of the house—usually a blessing—had curdled into something suffocating. She was an island, and the tide of the project was rising, and there was no one to hold the line with her. She closed her eyes, trying to exhale, but the air caught in her throat. She was working harder than she had ever worked in her life, and yet, she was moving backward, sinking into the sheer, crushing effort of existing alone in the center of a storm.
