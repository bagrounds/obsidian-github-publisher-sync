## The Cracks  
  
The Henderson account was a disaster of red ink and polite, soul-crushing disappointment. By 4:00 PM, the office was a blur of fluorescent hums and the quiet clicking of keyboards. Maya sat in her glass-walled office, the Henderson presentation splayed across her monitors like an autopsy of her own competence.   
  
Her boss, David, had been surgical. It wasn't just the lack of cohesion, he’d said, leaning against her doorframe with that maddening, calm neutrality. It’s the lack of conviction. It feels like you’re trying to design for a person who doesn't exist, Maya. Who are you designing for?  
  
She hadn’t had an answer. She had nodded, thanked him for the feedback, and watched him walk away with a steady, confident gait that made her own legs feel like they were made of damp paper.   
  
She opened her drawer and pulled out the small mirror she kept there. She inspected her face. The eyeliner was perfect. The skin was clear. The mask was holding, but the foundation was crumbling. She felt a sudden, sharp urge to smash the monitor, to let the failure spill out onto the floor, to be anything other than this polished, hollow shell.   
  
She texted Ethan. *Rough day. Can we talk later?*  
  
The three dots appeared almost instantly. *Babe, I’m headed to the gym for a pick-up game. Let’s do dinner at that Thai place? I’ll pick up some wine. Keep your chin up—you’re the queen of pivots!*  
  
She stared at the message. The emoji of the smiling face with sunglasses felt like an insult. He wasn't just avoiding the heavy stuff; he was actively denying the existence of gravity.   
  
She closed the mirror. She realized then that her perfectionism wasn't just a professional trait—it was a frantic attempt to ensure that Ethan would never have to see her fail. She believed, with the irrational, bone-deep certainty of a child, that if she could just keep the house, the career, and the marriage in a state of flawless equilibrium, she would be safe.   
  
She stood up and walked to the window. The city below was indifferent. She thought about the Henderson account, about the way her voice had faltered when David pressed her. She realized she wasn't just terrified of losing the account. She was terrified that if she told Ethan the truth—that she was drowning in a job she didn't even like, that she felt like a fraud who was about to be found out—he would look at her with that same bright, superficial optimism and try to fix it with a joke or a glass of wine.   
  
She needed a witness, not a cheerleader. But Ethan didn't know how to witness. He only knew how to perform.   
  
She picked up her bag, her movements slow and deliberate. She felt a crack in her chest—not a break, but a thin, jagged line where the pressure was finally beginning to escape. She walked out of the office, the lights flicking off behind her one by one, leaving the room in a cold, silent dark. As she walked to the elevator, she didn't check her reflection. For the first time, she didn't care what the world saw. She only cared about the silence waiting for her at home, and the terrifying, necessary conversation she was finally going to force.
