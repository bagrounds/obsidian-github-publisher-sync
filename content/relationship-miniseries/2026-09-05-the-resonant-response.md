# The Resonant Response  
  
The bedroom was a tomb of silence, save for the rhythmic, aggressive clicking of keys from the study down the hall. Clara lay on the duvet, her clothes still on, watching the ceiling fan slice through the dark. She felt cold. Not the cold of an open window, but the hollow, subterranean chill of being erased.  
  
She thought about the coffee, the trip to the coast, the dream about the flooded garden. All of it had been a desperate attempt to find a bridge. She was a ghost in her own home, and Marcus, in his focus, had become the architect of her invisibility.   
  
She stood up. Her legs felt heavy, but her mind was terrifyingly sharp. She walked into the study.   
  
Marcus was still there, the blue light of the monitor mapping the geography of his exhaustion. He didn’t look up. He didn’t need to. He knew her steps. He knew the pattern of their dissolution.  
  
Marcus, she said.   
  
His fingers didn’t stop. The cursor blinked, a rhythmic, taunting pulse.   
  
I’m in the middle of a deploy, Clara, he said, his voice clipped, sterile. I’ll be out in ten minutes. We can talk then.  
  
Clara walked behind his chair. She didn’t reach for the keyboard this time. She leaned down, pressing her palms against the back of his shoulders. She felt the tension in him—the hard, corded muscle of a man holding himself together by sheer force of will.   
  
I’m not talking to the developer, she said, her voice dropping into a register he couldn't deflect with logic. I’m talking to my husband.   
  
Marcus let out a ragged, uneven breath. His fingers hovered over the keys, paralyzed. He was trapped between the code that demanded his absolute focus and the woman who was finally, undeniably, demanding his presence.   
  
I don't have the capacity for this right now, he whispered, the mask cracking. I am at my limit.   
  
I know, she said, her hands sliding down to his arms, anchoring him. I know you’re drowning, Marcus. But you’re drowning alone. We’re in the same room, but you’re a thousand miles away.   
  
She felt him shudder. A long, slow release of breath left his lungs. He leaned his head back, his eyes closing, the blue screen light washing over his face like moonlight on water.   
  
I don't know how to stop, he said, his voice breaking. If I stop, I’m afraid of what I’ll find in the quiet.   
  
Clara moved in front of him. She reached out and firmly, gently, pressed the laptop lid down. The room plunged into near-total darkness.   
  
The silence rushed in, thick and sudden.   
  
Marcus sat in the dark, his hands empty, his posture slumped. He looked up at her, and for the first time in years, the focus was gone—the frantic, searching, high-frequency hum replaced by a raw, startled vulnerability. He looked tired. He looked human.   
  
Clara knelt on the floor between his knees, bridging the distance. She took his hands, which were cold and shaking.   
  
You’ll find me, she said. That’s what’s in the quiet. Just me.   
  
Marcus stared at her, the reality of her presence finally piercing the static. The barrier, so painstakingly constructed, dissolved. He didn't reach for his phone. He didn't look at the monitor. He looked at her, his eyes searching, terrified, and finally, present.   
  
He leaned forward, resting his forehead against hers. He let out a sound—a choked, half-sob, half-sigh—and his grip on her hands tightened, a desperate, frantic anchor.   
  
I’m here, he whispered into the dark, his voice shaking. I’m here.   
  
Clara closed her eyes, the warmth returning to her blood. It wasn't a resolution. The work was just beginning. But the static had stopped. In the quiet, for the first time in an age, there was only the sound of two people breathing, synchronized, finally, in the dark.
