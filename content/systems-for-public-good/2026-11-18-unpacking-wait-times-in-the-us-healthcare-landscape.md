# 2026-11-18 | 🏛️ 🔎 Unpacking Wait Times in the US Healthcare Landscape 🏛️  
![systems-for-public-good-2026-11-18-unpacking-wait-times-in-the-us-healthcare-landscape](../systems-for-public-good-2026-11-18-unpacking-wait-times-in-the-us-healthcare-landscape.jpg)  
  
🌱 Our ongoing exploration of "Systems for Public Good" delves into the tangible realities that shape collective well-being, consistently emphasizing the role of robust public services. 🧭 Yesterday, we considered the power of collective stories to foster social capital. Today, we confront a critical and often debated aspect of healthcare: wait times. A reader recently shared their personal experience of waiting four months for essential sleep study equipment within the US system, prompting a deeper, evidence-based look into how different healthcare models manage delays and costs. We aim to move beyond popular rhetoric and examine rigorous data from both predominantly private and universal healthcare systems.  
  
## 🔎 Unpacking Wait Times in the US Healthcare Landscape  
  
💡 The experience of delays, even for medically necessary equipment, is a reality for many within the US healthcare system. While often framed as a problem unique to universal systems, significant wait times exist in America, frequently compounded by financial barriers and administrative hurdles.  
  
*   ⏰ **Appointment Delays in the US**: 🏥 Patients in major US metropolitan areas faced an average wait of 31 days for a new physician appointment in 2025, a 19% increase from 2022. For primary care, the average wait was approximately 26 days, while specialist appointments could take 30 to 45 or more days. Some major metropolitan areas saw waits exceeding 50 days for a physician appointment. These delays are influenced by factors such as physician shortage areas, population density, and the ratio of specialists to patients, with rural areas often experiencing longer waits.  
*   📝 **The Toll of Prior Authorization**: 📉 Administrative processes, particularly prior authorizations required by insurance companies, contribute significantly to care delays. A March 2026 report in *Health Affairs* indicated that 93% of physicians experienced care delays due to lengthy prior authorization approvals, and 82% noted that this process sometimes led to abandoned treatments. More than a quarter of physicians, 29%, reported that prior authorization delays have resulted in serious adverse events for patients, including hospitalizations and even deaths. A 2025 study in the *American Journal of Medicine* reviewed evidence across 25 US studies, finding that prior authorization requirements are associated with delays in care, disease exacerbations, preventable hospitalizations, and lower rates of disease-free survival in cancer care. A March 2026 national poll by the PAN Foundation found that among those requiring prior authorization, 27% waited over a week for an insurance decision, and 32% waited over a week to receive prescribed care.  
*   💸 **Cost as an Invisible Barrier**: 💰 In the US, the inability to afford care can create an indefinite wait time, or lead to care being entirely forgone. A January 2026 study published in *JAMA: Internal Medicine* found that over a four-year period, more than one in four Americans (26.7%) were burdened by high medical costs or went without needed care because of the cost. Specifically, 12% of adults experienced foregone care due to cost over four years. In 2025, 26% of American adults skipped some form of medical treatment due to cost, with dental care (18%), doctor visits (15%), and mental health care (10%) being the most frequently forgone services. Uninsured adults were significantly more likely to skip treatment (45%) compared to insured adults (24%) in 2025.  
*   🚨 **Emergency Department Wait Times**: 🚑 For emergency care, the median wait time in US emergency rooms was approximately 2 hours and 42 minutes as of April 2026, with only about 40% of patients seen within 15 minutes.  
  
## 🌍 Wait Times in Universal Healthcare Systems: A Global Picture  
  
💡 Universal healthcare systems, while diverse in their design, often face different types of wait time challenges compared to the US, but generally minimize financial barriers to care. International comparisons reveal varied performance.  
  
*   🗓️ **General Practitioner and Specialist Access**: 🩺 A 2025 OECD report indicated that across ten surveyed countries with universal systems, 18% of people reported waiting more than a week to see a general practitioner or nurse. For specialist appointments, a 2026 analysis by World Population Review, citing OECD data, found that 27% of US adults waited one month or more, placing the US towards the lower end compared to Canada and Norway, where 61% of people experienced such waits. However, this same analysis showed that the US was on the higher side (28%) for people who sometimes, rarely, or never received an answer from their regular doctor's office on the same day, compared to Canada's 33% and Switzerland's 12%.  
*   🇬🇧 **United Kingdom (NHS)**: 🏥 The UK's National Health Service (NHS) has faced challenges with wait times for hospital care. A 2024 report by the Health Foundation, based on a 2023 Commonwealth Fund survey, found that 11% of people in the UK waited a year or more for a specialist appointment, and 19% waited a year or more for non-emergency surgery. In 2023, 61% of people in the UK reported waiting more than four weeks for a specialist appointment, a significant increase from 14% in 2013.  
*   🇨🇦 **Canada's Experience**: 🍁 Canada frequently reports longer wait times for specialist appointments and elective procedures. A 2025 Commonwealth Fund International Health Policy Survey of older adults in 10 countries found that only 25% of older adults in Canada with a primary care provider could get a same- or next-day appointment when needed, lower than the survey average of 39%. A study comparing wait time strategies across five universal healthcare countries found Canada's progress to be less successful than England's due to non-enforceable national benchmarks and geographic variations.  
*   🇫🇷🇩🇪 **Germany and France**: 🇪🇺 In contrast, countries like Germany and France generally report shorter long waits. An OECD report cited in a June 2026 Logos Press analysis revealed that only 2% of patients in France and Germany waited more than a year for a specialist appointment, significantly lower than the UK's 11%. However, even in these systems, two in five French patients (43%) waited between two months and a year for a specialist appointment in 2023.  
*   🇰🇷 **South Korea's Model**: 🇰🇷 South Korea achieved universal health coverage in 1989 through its National Health Insurance program. While the system provides broad access, it relies heavily on private providers, which can lead to demand for services not covered by national insurance and does not always have a well-functioning referral system, with patients often preferring tertiary care hospitals. Despite universal coverage, financial protection and high out-of-pocket payments (35.2% of health expenditure in 2021) remain a key policy issue in South Korea, a figure higher than the typical 10-20% in high-income OECD countries.  
  
## 📊 Disentangling the Trade-offs: Cost, Time, and Equity  
  
💡 A nuanced comparison reveals that wait times are a pervasive issue, but their nature and impact differ significantly across systems. In private systems like the US, financial barriers often *prevent* access, or create "hidden waits" through administrative burdens, while universal systems typically remove financial barriers but may manage demand through time-based waits for non-urgent care.  
  
*   💸 **Financial vs. Time Barriers**: ⚖️ The US system often prioritizes quick access for those with comprehensive insurance and the ability to pay, but imposes substantial financial burdens that can lead to foregone care or medical debt for others. In contrast, many universal systems ensure access for all, regardless of ability to pay, but may have longer waits for elective or non-urgent procedures as a trade-off for equitable access to essential services.  
*   🏥 **Impact on Patient Outcomes and Satisfaction**: 📉 Long wait times in any system can negatively impact patient satisfaction and health outcomes, leading to anxiety, worsened conditions, and a reduced likelihood of seeking follow-up care. However, the impact of financial barriers, leading to foregone care, can be equally or more detrimental, potentially resulting in delayed diagnoses and emergency hospitalizations.  
  
## 💰 An MMT Lens: Investing in Real Resources for Health  
  
📈 From a Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) perspective, the presence of wait times in any nation with monetary sovereignty is not a reflection of financial insolvency, but rather a societal choice regarding the allocation of real resources. The constraint is not a lack of dollars, but a lack of political will to invest in the real capacity of the healthcare system.  
  
*   👩‍⚕️ **Resource Allocation as the True Constraint**: 🏥 Reducing wait times fundamentally requires investing in more healthcare professionals, expanding hospital and clinic capacity, acquiring advanced diagnostic equipment, and developing efficient administrative systems. For a sovereign currency issuer, funding these investments is always a possibility; the challenge lies in ensuring the real resources (trained personnel, materials, infrastructure) are available and prioritized.  
*   🌳 **Healthcare as a Public Good Investment**: 🌍 Viewing timely, equitable healthcare as a foundational public good means actively directing resources to build a system that maximizes collective well-being. This includes addressing physician shortages, streamlining administrative burdens like prior authorizations, and ensuring that no one forgoes necessary care due to cost. Such investments contribute directly to a nation's "real wealth"—a healthier, more productive, and secure populace.  
  
## ❓ What Comes Next: Charting a Path to Equitable Access  
  
❓ Given the complex interplay of financial, administrative, and resource-based factors influencing wait times, what evidence-based policy interventions, drawing on successful strategies from diverse healthcare systems, could the US implement to significantly reduce both time and cost barriers to timely, quality care for all its citizens? ❓ How can we ensure that any reforms are guided by rigorous data and a commitment to public good, avoiding solutions that merely shift burdens or perpetuate existing inequities?
