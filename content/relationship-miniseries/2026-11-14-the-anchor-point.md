## The Anchor Point  
  
The final render of the library blueprints sat on the secondary monitor, the lines now crisp and coherent. The math had held. The load-bearing calculations, which had seemed like a descent into chaos forty-eight hours ago, now looked remarkably elegant.   
  
Clara stood by the window, watching the street. The light was shifting into the long, amber glow of late afternoon. She felt a profound, quiet exhaustion, the kind that comes after a long fever breaks. It wasn't the frantic, red-lined fatigue of earlier in the week; it was the heavy, satisfied tiredness of work well done.  
  
Leo was at the desk, cleaning his pens. He was quiet, his movements efficient and rhythmic. He didn't look up as she approached, but he shifted his chair just enough to make space for her.  
  
She sat on the edge of his workspace, her hip brushing his arm. The contact was so simple it was almost mundane, yet she felt the familiar, grounding shift in her own nervous system. The residual hum of the deadline—the phantom threat of the inspector’s red pen—vanished the moment she made contact.  
  
You know, she said, watching him cap a marker. I don't think I could have finished that structural model if you hadn't been in the room.  
  
Leo paused, his pen hovering over the sketchbook. He looked at her, his expression steady. I didn't do anything, Clara. I just sat here and drew.  
  
You did the most important thing, she replied. You were just there.  
  
She thought about the days before the deadline, when she had paced this room like a caged animal, convinced that the weight of the project was a singular, solitary burden she had to bear or collapse under. She had treated the project as a threat to her survival, and her brain had obligingly redlined in response.   
  
But then there had been the sound of his pencil. The sight of his silhouette. The deliberate, grounding weight of his hand on her back when the panic had crested.   
  
She realized now that her brain had been scanning for threats because it was designed to expect a partner. When she was alone, the world became a dangerous place. When she was with him, the world was just a place—a place to be worked in, to be navigated, to be lived. He had functioned as an external regulator, his own calm nervous system acting as a bridge for her to cross back into her own capacity.  
  
Leo turned fully toward her, setting the pen down. It’s funny, he said. I felt like I was just waiting for you to find your way back. I didn't know I was helping.  
  
You were the anchor, she said.  
  
She leaned into him, letting the tension finally drain out of her shoulders. She didn't need to explain the biology of it. She didn't need to tell him about the way her heart rate synced with his, or how the simple proximity of another human had shifted her perception of the structural load from impossible to calculated.   
  
The room felt different now. It wasn't a site of trial; it was a home. And the work wasn't a disaster waiting to happen; it was just lines on a screen.   
  
She took a deep, steady breath, the air filling the very bottom of her lungs, clean and unencumbered. She was tired, yes, but she was whole. She reached out, taking his hand, and held it. The anchor held. And in the quiet of the office, as the sun dipped below the horizon, there was finally nothing left to carry but the silence, and the strength of being together.
