# The Fracture Point  
  
The front door slammed, a concussive thud that rattled the framed prints in the hallway. Clara didn't bother to hang up her coat. She stood in the entryway, the silence of the apartment feeling like a physical weight against her chest.   
  
Marcus was in the study. The light was on, that familiar, thin line of illumination bleeding out into the darkened living room. He was on a call—she could hear the clipped, efficient cadence of his professional voice, the way he modulated his tone to project competence and detachment.  
  
Clara walked to the kitchen, her movements sharp and deliberate. She poured a glass of water, the sound of the tap screaming in the quiet. She knew what was coming. The weekend. The coast. The promise he had made and already forgotten.   
  
She walked to the study door and stood there. She didn't knock. She just entered.  
  
Marcus glanced up, his expression tightening—a momentary flicker of annoyance at the interruption. He held up a hand, a silent command for her to wait, to be quiet, to exist in the periphery.   
  
Clara watched him. She felt the static, that low-frequency hum of his distraction, vibrating in the very air of the room. She could see the fatigue around his eyes, the way his jaw was clenched, the sheer, exhausting effort he put into being everywhere else but here.   
  
She walked over to his desk. She reached out and placed her hand flat on his keyboard.   
  
Marcus froze. His fingers stopped mid-tap. He looked up at her, his eyes cold, his jaw set.   
  
Clara, he said, his voice a low, warning rasp. I am in the middle of a release.   
  
I don't care, she said, her voice shaking. I don't care about the release. I don't care about the logs. I don't care about your productivity. I am dying in this house, Marcus. I am literally disappearing, and you don't even see the empty chair next to you.  
  
Marcus didn't move. He didn't pull away, but he didn't lean in either. He simply looked at her hand on his keyboard, then up at her face. The silence stretched, brittle and sharp.   
  
You are being hysterical, he said, his voice devoid of heat, which somehow made it worse. You are taking your frustration with your own life and projecting it onto my work. I am doing this for us. I am building the structure of our life.   
  
The structure, she repeated, a hollow, bitter laugh escaping her. There is no structure, Marcus. There is only a cage. And I am the only one rattling the bars.   
  
She pulled her hand back, her skin feeling raw. She waited for him to reach for her, to apologize, to offer even a shred of acknowledgment. She held her breath, the space between them filled with the ghost of a thousand bids he had failed to hear.   
  
Marcus sighed—a long, weary sound—and turned his head back to the screen. The flickering blue light reflected in his pupils, a cold, unyielding barrier.   
  
I am going to finish this, he said, his fingers returning to the keys. We can talk about this when I’m off the clock.   
  
Clara stood there, watching the back of his head. She felt a profound, chilling clarity. The repair attempt hadn't just failed; it hadn't even been acknowledged. She had reached across the water, and he had simply turned his boat and rowed away into the dark.   
  
She turned and walked out of the study. She didn't look back. She went to the bedroom, closed the door, and lay down on the bed, staring at the ceiling in the dark, listening to the relentless, rhythmic clicking of his keys—the sound of a life being built, stone by stone, with her on the outside.
