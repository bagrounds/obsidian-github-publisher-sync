## The Familiar Chord  
  
The dinner table, once a place of Sunday roasts and lingering wine, had become a neutral zone of strictly observed boundaries. Sarah pushed a piece of rosemary chicken around her plate, the ceramic scraping—*skritch, skritch*—sound magnified by the silence of the kitchen.  
  
Daniel was reading a newsletter on his phone, his thumb flicking upward in a rhythmic, dismissive motion. He hadn't spoken since they sat down.   
  
Sarah looked at him. She saw the way his brow furrowed, the way he tilted the phone slightly away from her, as if shielding his screen from an intruder. Her internal narrative, a well-worn track she played on a loop, whispered that this wasn't just reading. It was a barricade. It was the familiar, chilling signal that she was no longer a priority, just a background fixture in his domestic architecture.  
  
You haven’t mentioned the kitchen sink, Sarah said. It was a simple observation, a practical follow-up to the plumber they’d had out on Tuesday.  
  
Daniel didn't look up. He didn't even pause his scrolling. Still dripping, he muttered, his eyes locked on the screen. I’ll look at it when I get a minute.  
  
The response was perfectly reasonable. It was factual. But Sarah didn't hear a status update. She heard the familiar echo of five years ago, the way he had dismissed her concerns about the foundation, the way he had once walked away while she was crying in the hallway. The *still dripping* wasn't about the faucet; it was an indictment of her own constant, leaking needs.   
  
She felt the sharp, cold spike of Negative Sentiment Override. It hit her like a physical tremor. Every neutral word Daniel uttered was now a carrier wave for his perceived indifference.   
  
I don’t know why I bother asking, she said, her voice dropping into a register of practiced, weary resignation. I should have known you’d just let it rot.  
  
Daniel’s thumb froze on the screen. He looked up then, his face shifting from concentration to that familiar, defensive hardening. He didn't hear a comment about plumbing. He heard a comprehensive critique of his character—a judgment he had been fighting against for years.   
  
I’m eating, Sarah, he said, his tone tight, calibrated to prevent an explosion. I told you I’d handle it. Why is it that everything I do is somehow a failure in your eyes?  
  
Sarah stared at him. She didn't see the man who was tired after a long day of site visits. She saw the ghost of every time he had shut her out, every time his silence had felt like a wall. The filter clicked into place, vivid and absolute.   
  
I didn’t say you were a failure, she said, the words heavy with the weight of the past. But I guess if the shoe fits.  
  
Daniel set the phone down. The screen glowed, illuminating the space between them—a chasm filled with the ghosts of arguments they had never truly resolved. He opened his mouth to explain, to defend, to de-escalate, but the words died in his throat. He knew, with a sudden, sinking dread, that no matter what he said, it would be heard as an attack.   
  
The record was playing again. And they were both trapped in the groove.
