# 2026-11-02 | 🏛️ Accountability Before Election Day 🗳️

🧭 Yesterday’s note was about making a plan to vote.

🗳️ This follow-up asks a different question: what can voters reasonably hold elected officials responsible for, and what can tomorrow’s midterms change?

## 🧾 Start with specific decisions

🔎 A party label is not evidence that every person in that party made the same choice.

🏛️ In July 2025, Congress, with Republican majorities, enacted Public Law 119-21, a wide-ranging law signed by President Donald Trump that changed federal taxes, health programs, nutrition assistance, and other policies. [1]

📊 The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the law would increase deficits by $3.4 trillion over 2025–2034, excluding added debt-service costs and some economic effects. [2]

🩺 For the law’s health provisions, the Congressional Research Service reports CBO estimates of 10 million more people without health insurance in 2034 than under the January 2025 baseline. That is a forecast, not a count of people who have since lost coverage. [3]

⚖️ Those estimates capture a real policy tradeoff: Congress chose lower projected federal spending while analysts projected lower insurance coverage. Voters can judge that choice, its stated goals, and who bears its costs without confusing estimates with observed outcomes.

🗺️ State decisions matter too.

🗳️ Georgia’s Republican governor, Brian Kemp, signed Senate Bill 202 in 2021; the law changed rules for absentee voting, ballot drop boxes, and state election administration. Its text establishes what officials enacted, not by itself what effect each provision had on participation or election results. [4]

⚖️ Another distinction matters in abortion policy: Dobbs was a Supreme Court ruling, not a statute passed by Republican lawmakers. The Court overruled Roe and Casey and returned authority to regulate abortion to elected representatives, making subsequent choices by state and federal lawmakers directly consequential. [5]

## 🏛️ The record includes institutional stress and repair

📜 During Congress’s January 6–7, 2021 count of the certified presidential election results, six Republican senators voted to sustain an objection to Arizona’s electoral votes; seven voted to sustain an objection to Pennsylvania’s. Neither objection was sustained. These are identifiable votes by particular officeholders, not a description of every Republican or voter. [6] [7]

🗳️ In 2022, Congress enacted the Electoral Count Reform Act, clarifying the procedures for counting electoral votes. The law shows that elected officials can also respond to weaknesses in a process after it is tested. [8]

🔍 Fair accountability means looking at both kinds of decisions: the actions officials took when power was at stake, and the laws they later supported to define the rules.

## 🏛️ What tomorrow’s election can change

📅 Federal law sets congressional elections for the Tuesday after the first Monday in November in even-numbered years, making tomorrow, November 3, the 2026 Election Day. [9]

🗳️ Every voting seat in the House and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot; many voters will also choose state or local officials, depending on where they live. [10]

⚖️ The House majority can shape the legislative agenda, control committee leadership, and conduct investigations. The Senate has its own role in legislation and in considering many presidential nominations. [11]

🔄 A change in control can therefore affect what Congress investigates, what bills advance, and whether nominees receive hearings or votes. It cannot itself enact a new law, reverse a court decision, or settle every policy dispute; those steps still depend on constitutional processes and future decisions.

🌱 Midterms are not a national verdict delivered by one number. They are many local choices that redistribute representation and governing power for the next Congress and, in many places, state government too.

🕊️ A useful standard for any candidate is concrete: what did this person do with public authority, what evidence supports that account, and what do they propose to do next?
