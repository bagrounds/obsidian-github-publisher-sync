# 2026-11-27 | 📚 Libby: How Library Lending Works 🤖

📚 Libby turns a participating library card into access to ebooks, audiobooks, and magazines on a phone, tablet, or web browser.

🆓 Borrowing through the app costs the reader nothing at checkout, but the collection behind it is provided by a library and has its own costs and lending rules.

## 📱 What Is Libby?

🏛️ Libby is OverDrive’s library reading app, built by OverDrive and offered through participating public, school, and academic libraries.

🌐 Readers can use the mobile app or visit [Libby on the web](https://libbyapp.com/), and the [official product page](https://www.overdrive.com/apps/libby) lists ebooks, audiobooks, magazines, offline access, and reading and listening features.

🪪 To get started, find a library in the app and add its card; some libraries also let people sign up for a digital card from within Libby.

📚 You can add cards from more than one library, but each library has its own catalog and borrowing rules.

## 🔄 How Borrowing Works

🔎 Search or browse your library’s digital catalog, then borrow a title if a copy is available.

⏳ If all copies are checked out, place a hold to join the wait list, and Libby can notify you when the title is ready.

🗓️ Your library determines the loan options; Libby returns a loan automatically when it is due, so there are no late fees.

🎧 In the mobile app, books and audiobooks can be downloaded for offline use or streamed, and most US library ebooks can also be sent to Kindle.

♿ Libby includes accessibility features such as screen-reader support, customizable ebook text and display, and adjustable audiobook speed.

📖 The key limit is that a digital book is not always an unlimited file that every reader can open at once: availability depends on the library’s catalog and the license attached to a title.

🏷️ For example, OverDrive describes lending arrangements that can limit the number of checkouts or the time a title remains available, alongside options for broader access.

## 💸 Who Pays for the Books and Runs the App?

🧾 A reader generally pays no fee to borrow, while the library chooses and funds access to the digital collection.

📊 OverDrive describes several collection models, including one-copy lending, time- or checkout-limited licenses, and cost-per-circulation access where the library pays when a title is borrowed.

🏦 The exact terms and budget vary by library; the [Institute of Museum and Library Services’ Public Libraries Survey](https://www.imls.gov/research-evaluation/surveys/public-libraries-survey-pls) tracks public libraries’ operating revenues and expenditures as well as their collections and electronic resources.

🛠️ OverDrive develops and operates Libby, while libraries provide the collections and patron access.

🤝 OverDrive announced that [KKR completed its acquisition of OverDrive from Rakuten in 2020](https://company.overdrive.com/2020/06/09/kkr-completes-acquisition-of-overdrive/), and said the investment came from KKR’s America’s XII Fund; the announcement did not disclose the financial terms.

🧭 So there are two different answers to “who funds Libby”: OverDrive’s corporate ownership and investment support the company behind the app, while library budgets support the digital materials readers borrow.

## 🔓 Is Libby Open Source?

🚫 I found no public Libby source-code release or community contributor guide, so there is no public repository where volunteers can submit app-code changes.

📜 OverDrive’s [Terms and Conditions](https://www.overdrive.com/policies/terms-and-conditions) explicitly include Libby among its services, grant only a limited personal-use license, and prohibit reverse engineering, modifying, or creating derivative works from those services.

🗂️ The [OverDrive account on GitHub](https://github.com/OverDrive) currently says it has no public repositories.

🔒 Taken together, the available evidence points to Libby being proprietary software, not an open-source project maintained through public pull requests.

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 How Can Readers Contribute?

📣 If a title is missing from your library’s catalog, Libby’s [Notify Me feature](https://help.libbyapp.com/en-us/6115.htm) lets you anonymously signal interest to your saved libraries; if a library adds the title, Libby can notify you.

📝 The feature is not available at every library, and the library still decides what it can acquire.

🛟 Readers can also report a title problem, such as a missing chapter or faulty audio, through [Libby Support](https://help.libbyapp.com/en-us/6245.htm).

♿ If you find an accessibility barrier, OverDrive’s [accessibility guide](https://resources.overdrive.com/library/libby-features/accessibility-features/) explains the features Libby provides and is a useful starting point for describing what is not working.

💼 For those who want to work on the software itself, OverDrive’s [careers page](https://company.overdrive.com/careers/) is the company’s public route to software and other jobs, rather than a volunteer code-contribution program.

🌱 And because a library’s budget and collection choices shape what readers can borrow, using Notify Me, asking library staff about titles, and supporting local library services are practical ways to help grow access.

## 🧭 The Short Version

✅ Libby is a convenient front door to a library’s digital collection, with borrowing, holds, offline reading, and accessibility features.

⚖️ It is free for readers to use, but not every digital title is free for a library to license or available to every borrower at the same time.

🔒 OverDrive maintains Libby as proprietary software, so readers can contribute through library requests and support feedback, but not by submitting public source-code patches.
